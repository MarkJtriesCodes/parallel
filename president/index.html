How this model works

This is an annual simulation of the US economy built from standard, well-documented macroeconomic relationships — the same building blocks the Congressional Budget Office and Federal Reserve staff use, radically simplified so it runs live in your browser.

Starting conditions (approximate 2026 baseline)

  • Real GDP growth ≈ 2% (CBO potential-growth estimate) · Unemployment ≈ 4.2% (BLS)
  • CPI inflation ≈ 2.7% (BLS) · Federal funds rate ≈ 4% · 10-yr Treasury ≈ 4.3%
  • Deficit ≈ 6% of GDP; debt held by the public ≈ 100% of GDP (CBO Budget & Economic Outlook)
  • Median existing-home price ≈ $420k (NAR) · 30-yr mortgage ≈ 6.4% (Freddie Mac)
  • Real median household income ≈ $83k, poverty ≈ 11%, Gini ≈ 0.48 (Census)
  • Average effective tariff starts at ~12%, reflecting 2025-26 trade actions (Yale Budget Lab estimates)

Core mechanisms

  • Okun's law: each 1pp of output gap moves unemployment ≈ 0.45pp the other way.
  • Phillips curve: inflation = persistence + expectations + ~0.3 × output gap + direct price shocks (tariffs, energy, wages). Expectations stay anchored only while the Fed is independent and credible.
  • Taylor rule: an independent Fed moves rates toward ~neutral + 1.5×(inflation − target) + 0.5×gap. If you seize rate-setting, whatever gap you leave between your rate and that benchmark stimulates or strangles the economy — and un-anchors expectations.
  • Fiscal multipliers: CBO ranges — roughly 0.4–1.5 depending on the instrument, bigger with slack, smaller when the Fed offsets.
  • Debt dynamics: debt/GDP grows with (interest − growth) plus the primary deficit; each pp of debt/GDP adds ≈ 2.5bp to long rates (Laubach 2009).
  • Housing: prices follow income, mortgage rates, and the supply of homes; supply levers act slowly, rate levers act fast.
  • Fragility index: a 0–100 heuristic for financial-crisis risk (capital rules, deregulation, monetized deficits, debt spirals). Above ~55 it starts costing growth and raising spreads — in reality this risk arrives suddenly, not smoothly.

Where the effect sizes come from

Every lever cites its sources in the ⓘ panel — including CBO and JCT scores, Federal Reserve research, and named peer-reviewed studies (Card & Krueger and Cengiz et al. on minimum wages; Amiti-Redding-Weinstein on tariffs; Diamond-McQuade-Qian on rent control; Chodorow-Reich on corporate taxes; the NAS consensus report on immigration; Fieldhouse & Mertens on R&D; Laubach on deficits and rates; Sargent on monetized deficits, and more). Each effect is entered as a range [small, central, large]; the three runs produce the shaded uncertainty band.

What this model honestly cannot do

  • Predict recessions' timing — real crises are sudden; the model smooths them into drags and warnings.
  • Capture politics: Congress, courts, and states constrain every one of these levers. You asked to be all-powerful, so here you are.
  • Price things GDP misses: health benefits of clean air, security value of export controls, lives changed by insurance — flagged in the evidence notes where they matter.
  • Settle genuinely open questions (AI's trajectory, exact minimum-wage thresholds) — those levers carry wide bands and say the evidence is thin.

Interactions are linearized: two policies together roughly add. The real economy is nonlinear, adaptive, and run by 340 million people who will surprise you.
